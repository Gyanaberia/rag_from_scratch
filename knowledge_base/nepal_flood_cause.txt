On the morning of 26 August 2026, a series of flash floods and debris flows, also known as the Nepal-China floods,[6][7] Bhotekoshi flood,[8][9] or Rasuwa flood,[10][11] destroyed the Gyirong checkpoint on the China–Nepal border and struck dozens of settlements along a 72 km (45 mi)[12] stretch of the Trishuli River[c] in Nepal.
At least 1,357 deaths and 5,326 missing persons in Nepal, and 43 deaths and 519 missing in the Tibet Autonomous Region of China were reported.[3][4] The flood carried the bodies of some victims 240 km (150 mi) away into neighbouring India.
Authorities have issued flood warnings for communities along rivers in the Nepali districts of Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading, Gorkha, and Chitwan.[13]
The precipitating geological event, thought to have been a glacier collapse on Langtang Lirung in the Himalayas, caused a chain reaction whose tremors were detected by seismometers worldwide, with magnitudes reaching Ms 5.2.[14][15]

Background
Geography and history
The Trishuli River[c] is joined by the Lhende Khola (also known as Lende River or Donglin Tsangpo in Lhasa Tibetan), which originates in Gyirong County, Tibet Autonomous Region before entering Nepal. It then flows past Dhunche, downstream from which it forms part of the Trishuli River system.[16][17]
The flash floods and mudslides occurred around the China–Nepal border crossing at Gyirong Port, a strategic border crossing connecting Nepal's Rasuwa District and Gyirong County, located at the confluence of the Trishuli and Lhende Khola rivers. The crossing saw large amounts of daily traffic related to both commerce and tourism, especially the pilgrimage tour to Mount Kailash, a holy site in Bon, Buddhism, Hinduism and Jainism.[18] The Gyirong crossing accounted for 30% of all Nepal-China trade before the disaster, and more than 100,000 people had crossed the crossing in the first half of 2026 according to Chinese media.[19]
Since 1900, dozens of floods have occurred near the border between Nepal and China.[20] Langtang Lirung, opposite the site of the 2026 landslide, experienced a major glacier collapse and debris avalanche triggered by the April and May 2015 Nepal earthquakes. The landslide in the Langtang valley destroyed and buried several villages, killing over 350 people.[21] A July 2025 flood caused by a glacial lake outburst washed away the bridge at the border crossing.[22][23]
Analysis of satellite imagery found that from 8 January 2026 to 18 August 2026, the glacier north of Langtang Lirung moved around 10 millimetres per month, but the rate accelerated weeks prior to the glacier collapse

Cause
A glacier collapse on the north face of Langtang Lirung, a mountain in Langtang National Park, Nepal, is widely thought to be the proximate cause of the floods (view from south-east).[citation needed]
No rain was reported on the morning of 26 August at the Rasuwa district headquarters. Nepal's foreign minister Shisir Khanal initially said that an earthquake triggered an avalanche which caused the flooding.[37] However, later analysis by the United States Geological Survey (USGS) indicated that the seismic signal did not precede the collapse but was rather generated by the collapse itself, meaning it was not a tectonic earthquake.[38] Other early analyses suspected a glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF).[39][40]
A seismic event measuring Ms 5.2 occurred at 02:52 UTC (08:37 NPT; 10:52 BJT) and was centred just north of Langtang Lirung, a mountain in Langtang National Park.[41] The USGS' analysis of long-period seismic waves demonstrated, however, that they were consistent with a glacier collapse, not an earthquake.[42] The GFZ Helmholtz Centre for Geosciences (GFZ), in Potsdam, Germany, recorded an event measuring Mw 5.7 that was consistent with a landslide with a timestamp identical to the USGS.[1]
A second landslide event was identified by USGS three hours after the first by analyzing low-frequency seismic waves. The second landslide was recorded as a Ms 4.2 seismic event.[43]
Seven minutes after (vis. 2026 Nepal floods timeline) the seismic event, a surveillance camera[44][45][46] captured floodwaters destroying the Gyirong Port border crossing.[47][48] Such was the scale and speed of the flood, the leading hypothesis of geomorphologists is that the collapsed portion of a glacier fell thousands of metres[29] and melted from the velocity and friction of the impact. The meltwater and rocks then displaced the river in the valley.[49][50]
There were indications of heavy water flow almost immediately after the seismic waves were detected. The combination of the two near-simultaneous signals, and review of the first satellite imagery, led researchers to conclude that a glacier located around 64 kilometres (40 mi) north of the capital city Kathmandu and some 15 km (9.3 mi) east of where the flood was first noticed, measuring 600 metres (2,000 ft) in width may have fallen 1,200 metres (4,000 ft),[26][51] in what is known as a glacier collapse (also called an ice avalanche).[52] According to Daniel Shugar, a geologist at the University of Calgary in Canada who reviewed satellite imagery after the event, a 0.2 km2 (49-acre) piece of ice and rock broke off at about 5.2 km (3.2 mi) above sea level and fell vertically about 1.2 km (0.75 mi) to the valley below.[53][54]
Satellite images and seismology confirm that a glacier collapsed near Langtang Lirung,[55] which itself is a likely result of destabilisation by tectonic uplift and climate warming.[56] Compared to GLOFs, glacier collapse floods are less frequent in the region, but more destructive.[57] The collapsed glacial material was believed to have originated from an altitude of 5,200–5,400 m (17,100–17,700 ft)[58] and had a size of around 197,000,000–492,000,000 m3 (7.0×109–1.74×1010 cu ft).[55] It then moved north-westwards before shifting west.[26]
The debris flowed up to some 100 km (62 mi) downstream.[26]

Risk of further flooding
Two barrier lakes formed following the glacier collapse.[59] On 27 August, Chinese authorities warned that one of the lakes with a volume of 2,000,000 m3 (71,000,000 ft3) had formed near the confluence of the Chhochen Khola and Purepu Tsangpo rivers, adding that the possibility of a breach was high due to an expected water inflow of 3,000,000 m3 (110,000,000 ft3) until 30 August.[60] The lake overflowed slightly on 28 August, prompting Chinese authorities to briefly pause rescue operations and warn about the potential for additional flooding.[61][62] The barrier lake slowly drained out and was largely empty by 30 August.[30] The other lake was situated at a high elevation, near where the glacier collapsed.[63] Flood warning notices were sent on 30 August after Chinese authorities reported increased water flow upstream.[30]
